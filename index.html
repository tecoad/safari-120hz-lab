Liquid Glass at 120 Hz in iOS Safari

A reproduction of the elements from “Bypassing iOS Safari’s 60 FPS Limit” by Rida F’kih.

This prototype rebuilds three pieces of the original article: the reading-progress indicator in the top-left corner, the contents menu in the top-right corner, and the tab bar further down. All three are glass drawn by a WebGL2 shader running in a worker. Open it in Safari on an iPhone to see the difference.

Scroll and watch the indicator: the text under it bends near the edge, with colored fringes. Tap the list button: it grows into a frosted glass panel, and the table of contents inside it is drawn by the shader too.

The problem: everything is stuck at 60 Hz

The iPhone has a 120 Hz display, but Safari caps websites at 60. requestAnimationFrame runs 60 times per second, touch events arrive 60 times per second, and the DOM is painted 60 times per second. Dragging something on a web page feels heavier than in a native app.

Where the limit lives

The cap applies to the main thread, where the page’s JavaScript runs. Two things escape it: native scrolling, which runs in the compositor, and the frames of an OffscreenCanvas, which don’t wait for the page’s rendering update. The whole trick is to use those two exits.

Input: native scrolling as a sensor

Instead of listening to touch events, each control has an invisible scroller under the finger. iOS itself does the dragging, at 120 Hz. The code only has to read scrollLeft and scrollTop fast enough.

Reading faster than the events

The scroll event fires only a few times per second during momentum. The fix is a custom loop: a MessageChannel schedules a setTimeout for every half frame at 120 Hz, aligned to the latest vsync timestamp. Because the timer is created inside a message event, it escapes the 4 ms clamp on nested timers. 

const slot = 1000 / 120 / 2
next = vsync + Math.ceil((now - vsync + 1) / slot) * slot - 1
setTimeout(read, next - now)

You can measure it. Drag the grid on the right: it is a native scroller, and the panel shows how many fresh readings arrive per second, how many of them carry a different position, and how fast the finger moves. The panel is drawn in a worker; otherwise it would be stuck at 60 Hz itself.

The bottleneck between processes

Safari’s UI process only sends a new scroll position after the previous one has been acknowledged. Positions in between are lost. An empty requestAnimationFrame loop keeps a rendering update queued, and with that WebKit flushes the position without waiting.

Keeping ProMotion awake

The display only goes up to 120 Hz when something needs it. While a finger is on the screen, an 8 px scroller smooth-scrolls back and forth every 250 ms, and a 1 px element runs an infinite CSS animation. Both stop one second after the last touch.

Sub-point precision

scrollLeft is rounded to an integer. The scrollers are created at their size multiplied by the device pixel ratio and scaled back down with transform: scale(), so each scroll unit is worth one physical pixel.

Output: OffscreenCanvas

Reading the finger at 120 Hz doesn’t help if the screen only shows 60 frames. With canvas.transferControlToOffscreen(), the canvas becomes a placeholder and the drawing happens in a worker. WebKit presents those frames without waiting for the page’s rendering update, and the glass reaches 120 fps.

This behavior is undocumented. Apple could change it without violating the specification.

Glass without reading pixels

A page can’t read its own pixels, so the glass has nothing to refract. The fix is to redo the layout: the DOM becomes a list of words and rectangles with their positions, and the worker rasterizes only the band near the screen into a ring-buffer texture. The shader samples that texture with refraction, chromatic aberration, and blur.

  • Refraction: near the edge, the sample moves along the shape’s normal.
  • Aberration: the red and blue channels move a little less and a little more.
  • Frost: the region under the panel is downsampled and run through a separable Gaussian.
  • Finish: inner shade, a bevel highlight, and a thin line just outside the edge.

The tab bar

Each tab is a native scroller with 97 snap cells. On touch, the lens grows and lifts. On drag, it follows the finger and squashes with speed. On release, it settles on the nearest tab. Pulling past the bar stretches the glass toward the finger, and the search button fuses with the bar when the two get close.

State machine

The controls only send timestamped events: pressed, moved, pulled, released. Everything else is derived from them on every frame: the lens position, how far it has lifted, how far the bar has stretched, and how visible the pill is. The springs use Apple’s format, a damping ratio and a response time.

Shaders

The bar, the button, the pill, and the lens are shapes defined by signed distance. The bar and the button merge with a polynomial smooth union. The icons come from a two-channel texture: red holds the outline and green holds the filled version, which only shows under the pill or the lens.

Limitations

All of this depends on undocumented WebKit behavior. The original author doesn’t recommend rebuilding a whole app on top of it and would rather Apple supported ProMotion for websites natively. This is a study: the goal is to understand the pieces.

Outside Safari, the loop uses a plain requestAnimationFrame and the display boost stays off. Without WebGL2 or OffscreenCanvas, the menu falls back to a DOM version with backdrop-filter.
